CHAPTER 1: THE TURKEY

                      BLUE
                (confident)
          Step one, the turkey. You cook it till
          it's done. Boom. Chapter over.

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          Done is a temperature, not a feeling.
          The USDA says one sixty-five in the
          thickest part of the breast, the
          thigh, and the wing.

                      BLUE
                (confused)
          Wait, the whole bird isn't the same
          temperature?

                      ORANGE
          Nope. A turkey is two meats wearing
          one coat. White meat in the breast,
          dark meat in the legs.

                      ORANGE
          The breast is lean. Push it much past
          one sixty-five and it dries out. The
          thighs are full of connective tissue,
          and they actually get better around
          one seventy to one seventy-five, when
          that tissue melts into gelatin.

                      BLUE
                (gasps)
          So the turkey wants two different
          temperatures at the same time?

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          Like every family at this table. Yes.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          Why is the leg meat even dark? Did it
          get a tan?

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          No tan. Legs do the walking. Muscles
          that work all day need oxygen on hand,
          so they're packed with a protein
          called myoglobin, and myoglobin is
          red.

                      ORANGE
          The breast barely works, since turkeys
          hardly fly. Less myoglobin, paler
          meat, and less fat to protect it.
          That's why it dries out first.

                      BLUE
                (gasps)
          So the breast is lazy and the legs are
          athletes.

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          Exactly. And in the oven, the lazy
          part is on top, closest to the heat.

                      BLUE
                (nervous)
          Can we just... flip the turkey's whole
          life around?

                      ORANGE
          Kind of. You can spatchcock it. Cut
          out the backbone and press the bird
          flat, so the legs sit out at the edges
          where it's hottest and the breast lies
          flat and thin.

                      ORANGE
          Everything cooks more evenly, and a
          lot faster. It's the closest thing to
          fixing the two-temperature problem.

                      BLUE
                (delighted)
          Spatchcock. That's my new favorite
          word.

                      BLUE
                (laughs)
          Then that's why the button is genius.
          It knows.

                      ORANGE
                (sighs)
          Let me show you the button. Inside,
          there's a spring, pushed down and held
          by a tiny blob of soft metal.

                      ORANGE
          When that metal gets hot enough, it
          melts, lets go of the spring, and the
          red stick shoots up. The idea came
          from fire sprinklers, which work the
          same way.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          And it melts at one sixty-five, right?

                      ORANGE
                (flatly)
          Most of them are set to pop around one
          eighty to one eighty-five. By then the
          breast is way past one sixty-five.
          That's your dry turkey.

                      BLUE
                (offended)
          I trusted that button with my life.

                      ORANGE
          And there's a second trap. When you
          take a turkey out of the oven, it
          keeps cooking. The outside is still
          hotter than the middle, so heat keeps
          moving inward.

                      ORANGE
          A big roast can climb another ten
          degrees or more while it rests. So if
          you pull it right at one sixty-five,
          you're actually serving it hotter.

                      BLUE
                (amazed)
          So the turkey cooks itself on the
          counter?

                      ORANGE
          Basically. Use a real thermometer,
          pull it a little early, and let it
          rest under foil. The rest also lets
          the juices settle, so they end up in
          the meat, not on the cutting board.

                      BLUE
                (proud)
          Thermometer. Rest. Ignore the button.
          I'm a genius.

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          You're a genius who was wrong three
          times in one chapter.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          Fine. But the turkey's the hard part,
          right?

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          The turkey isn't even the hardest
          thing on this table.


CHAPTER 1B: THE NIGHT BEFORE

                      BLUE
                (confident)
          Fine, but the turkey's still frozen.
          I'll leave it on the counter tonight.
          Thawed by morning.

                      ORANGE
                (flatly)
          Thawed on the outside. Still an
          iceberg in the middle.

                      ORANGE
          Bacteria grow fastest between forty
          and one forty degrees. On the counter,
          the outside sits in that zone for
          hours while the inside is still frozen
          solid.

                      BLUE
                (gasps)
          So my turkey is a bacteria hotel.

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          All-inclusive. Thaw it in the fridge.
          The USDA says about one day for every
          four to five pounds. A big bird needs
          most of a week.

                      BLUE
                (panicking)
          A week? Thanksgiving is in, like, a
          few days!

                      ORANGE
          Then do it in cold water, sealed in
          the bag, and change the water every
          thirty minutes. Roughly half an hour

                      BLUE
                (relieved)
          Phew. Then I'll stuff it the night
          before, so it's ready to go.

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          Two problems. Stuffing soaks up raw
          turkey juice. To be safe, the stuffing
          in the middle has to hit one sixty-
          five too.

                      ORANGE
          And the middle of the bird is the last
          place to heat up. By the time the
          stuffing is safe, the breast is way
          overcooked again.

                      BLUE
                (sighs)
          The turkey keeps setting traps.

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          So bake the stuffing in its own dish.
          It gets crispy edges, and nobody gets
          sick.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          What about brining? My neighbor soaks
          his turkey in a bucket of salt water.

                      ORANGE
          Your neighbor is onto something. Salt
          loosens some of the muscle proteins,
          so the meat holds on to more water
          while it cooks. Juicier breast, and a
          little more room for error.

                      BLUE
                (proud)
          So salt is basically turkey insurance.

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          Salt is turkey insurance. The button

CHAPTER 2: THE PIE

                      BLUE
                (confident)
          The pie, then. Pie is easy. You put
          pie in the oven until it looks like
          pie.

                      ORANGE
          Pumpkin pie isn't really a cake. It's
          a custard. Eggs, milk, pumpkin. The
          eggs are what make it set.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          Set like jelly?

                      ORANGE
          Kind of. When egg proteins heat up,
          they unravel and link into a soft net
          that holds the filling together.
          That's the smooth, creamy slice.

                      ORANGE
          But if they keep heating, that net
          keeps tightening. It squeezes, it
          shrinks, and the top splits right down
          the middle.

                      BLUE
                (gasps)
          That's why grandma's pie always has
          the Grand Canyon in it.

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          Respectfully, yes. The fix feels
          wrong: pull the pie while the center
          still wobbles a little. The edges
          should look set, the middle should
          jiggle like soft gel.

                      ORANGE
          Just like the turkey, the heat keeps
          moving into the center after it leaves
          the oven. The wobble finishes on its
          own as it cools.

                      BLUE
                (nervous)
          Taking it out when it looks
          unfinished? That's terrifying.

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          That's baking.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          And the bottom?  Mine is always soggy.
          Like a wet napkin.

                      ORANGE
          Wet filling poured into raw dough
          soaks in before the crust has a chance
          to set. So bake the empty crust first,
          then add the filling. It's called
          blind baking.

                      BLUE
                (impressed)
          Blind baking. Sounds dangerous. I love
          it.

                      ORANGE
                (flatly)
          It's baking with the lights on. You
          just weigh the empty crust down so it
          doesn't puff up.

                      BLUE
                (confident)
          Got it. Wobble, blind bake. And to be
          safe, I'll leave it in a little
          longer.

                      ORANGE
                (sighs)
          That's the exact thing that cracks it.

                      BLUE
                (excited)
          The sides! Gravy first. I'll just dump

                      ORANGE

                      BLUE

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          They taste like raw flour. The fix is
          to split the starch up before it gets
          hot. Whisk it into cold liquid first,
          or cook it in the fat first, like a
          roux. Every grain gets its own space,
          so it thickens smooth.

                      BLUE
                (curious)
          Fine. Then mashed potatoes. I'll use
          the blender. Maximum smooth.

                      ORANGE
                (flatly)
          Maximum glue. Potato cells are little
          bags of starch. Cooking makes the
          starch swell. A few burst cells make
          it creamy.

                      ORANGE
          But a blender bursts almost all of
          them. All that loose starch turns into
          a sticky, stretchy paste. You made
          wallpaper paste.

                      BLUE
                (gasps)
          My potatoes are wallpaper?

                      ORANGE
                (deadpan)
          Your potatoes could hold up wallpaper.
          Mash gently, by hand, and stop when
          it's smooth.

                      BLUE
                (suspicious)
          And the cranberry sauce? How does that
          wiggle without any gelatin?

                      ORANGE
          Cranberries are packed with pectin,
          the same stuff that sets jam. Cook
          them with sugar and the pectin builds
          a gel on its own.

                      BLUE
                (amazed)
          The cranberries brought their own
          Jell-O.

                      ORANGE
                (dry)
          Pretty much. Now for the part everyone
          blames on the turkey.
