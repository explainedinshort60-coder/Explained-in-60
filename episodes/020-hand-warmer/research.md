## Sources
Note: Wikipedia, Scientific American, ACS ChemMatters and the HotHands FAQ were not fetchable this run (fetch permission prompt with nobody to answer). The script uses only claims the readable sources support.

- Field & Stream, "How do hand warmers work?", Allie Conti, 2026-01-23: https://www.fieldandstream.com/gear/how-do-hand-warmers-work
  - Exothermic reaction triggered by oxygen: "the same reaction that creates rust, just accelerated by chloride ions acting as a catalyst."
  - Ingredients: "powdered iron, chloride ions (also known as salts), charcoal, and some sort of absorbent material" (polyacrylate, vermiculite or pulverized wood).
  - Charcoal spreads the heat evenly across the packet.
  - Activates only when opened and oxygen gets in.
  - "up to 135 degrees Fahrenheit"; "can last between 7 and 24 hours".
  - Shaking makes it heat up faster (but it may not last as long).
  - In an airtight bag like a Ziploc "the iron will stop reacting"; opening it again restarts it.
  - The iron is toxic if swallowed: keep used ones from kids and pets.
- Duke University CHEM 110 (LibreTexts), "Thermochemistry of Hand Warmers in Everyday Life": https://chem.libretexts.org/Courses/Duke_University/CHEM_110_Honors_Writing_Projects/Duke_CHEM_110_Fall_2022:_Cox_and_Shorb/Thermochemistry_of_Hand_Warmers_in_Everyday_Life
  - Contents: iron powder, activated carbon, salt, an absorbent such as vermiculite. Salt is a catalyst, the absorbent retains moisture, carbon disperses heat, iron is the heat source.
  - Opening the package lets the iron contact oxygen: 2Fe + 3/2 O2 -> Fe2O3 (rust). The pouch limits the oxygen so it stays comfortably warm.
- KTTC (Rochester MN), "Looking into the science on how hand warmers work", 2025-02-23, quoting Dr. Marie Pomije, Minnesota State University, Mankato: https://www.kttc.com/2025/02/23/looking-into-science-how-hand-warmers-work
  - "Each packet is covered in a porous material for airflow."
  - Oxygen entering the packet reacts with iron powder to make iron oxide (rust).
  - Salt, water and activated carbon are inside with the iron; carbon "evenly disperses the heat"; charcoal or vermiculite controls the water.
  - Shaking lets more oxygen in, more rust, more heat.
- PBS Rough Science (WETA), hand warmers: https://www.pbs.org/weta/roughscience/series3/ice/handwarmers.html
  - "the reaction between iron and moist air that produces rust is a very exothermic process" (a rusting nail does it too, only slowly).
