## Claims and sources
| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Your lungs are a very damp place | University of Illinois Physics Van, "Seeing Your Breath": "Inside your lungs is a very damp place, so when you breath out, your breath has a lot of water in it." https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/738 |
| Every breath out is warm and full of invisible water vapor | Farmers' Almanac (updated 2026-10-21): "The air leaving your lungs is warm and loaded with moisture"; Mental Floss (2020-01-08): "we also exhale a certain amount of water vapor". Water vapor is an invisible gas (Physics Van: the vapor condenses into liquid droplets, which is what you see). https://www.farmersalmanac.com/why-can-you-see-your-breath ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/weather/why-you-can-see-your-breath |
| Outside, it hits the cold air and loses heat fast | Library of Congress blog (2021-12): "Cold air causes the warm moisture in our breath to condense into tiny droplets"; Farmers' Almanac: "Temperature determines how quickly your warm breath cools". https://blogs.loc.gov/families/2021/12/everyday-winter-mysteries-fun-science-facts-from-the-library/ |
| Once it cools to its dew point, some vapor turns into liquid | Farmers' Almanac: "Once it cools enough to reach its dew point, some of the invisible water vapor condenses into thousands of tiny liquid droplets." Mental Floss: vapor "condenses into a liquid when it hits dew point". |
| Thousands of tiny water droplets, that's what you see | Farmers' Almanac (above); Physics Van: "the water vapor in your breath will condense, changing from a gaseous vapor into tiny liquid water droplets"; LOC: "tiny droplets of water that appear like a small, misty cloud". |
| A very small, very temporary cloud | Farmers' Almanac: "a very small, very temporary cloud". |
| Real clouds and fog form the same way | Farmers' Almanac: the same process forms clouds and fog; Mental Floss: condensation "is also how clouds are formed". |
| A second later the droplets evaporate again | Farmers' Almanac: "Your breath cloud just lasts a second or two before those droplets evaporate and disappear." |
| It does not have to be freezing; on a humid morning you can see it in the forties | Farmers' Almanac: "you don't have to be anywhere near 32°F to see your breath ... it can happen when the temperature is in the 40s"; "On a cool, humid morning, you may notice your breath while temperatures are still comfortably in the 40s." |
| Humidity matters too, not just temperature; on a dry day you may not see it | Mental Floss: "it's actually relative humidity, not just temperature, that determines whether you can see your breath"; "Dry, unsaturated air can hold more water vapor, so you can huff and puff without seeing any evidence of it at all." Farmers' Almanac: "Humidity matters too." |

Not claimed: any breath temperature in degrees, any single threshold number (45 F is only secondhand), ice crystals, that the droplets are smoke. The dragon gag is Blue's joke and Orange shuts it down.
