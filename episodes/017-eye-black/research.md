## Claims and sources
| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| The idea: sunlight hits your cheekbones and bounces up into your eyes; that is glare | Framed as "the idea is" (the purpose eye black is meant for). Wikipedia "Eye black": "a grease or strip applied under the eyes to reduce glare". Mental Floss (W. McGough, 2013-08-20): black absorbs most light, so stray light around the eye is absorbed and contrast on the object rises. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye_black ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/52181/how-effective-eye-black-athletes-wear |
| Glare washes out the ball against a bright sky | Yale release: sun glare reduces contrast sensitivity, the ability to pick an object out from its background. https://medicine.yale.edu/news-article/3205 |
| Black grease soaks up that light instead of bouncing it | Mental Floss: "the color black absorbs most light frequencies". Yale release: eye black grease is "a combination of beeswax and pigment". |
| In 2003, Yale doctors tested it in bright sunlight | DeBroff BM, Pahk PJ (Yale ophthalmology), "The ability of periorbitally applied antiglare products to improve contrast sensitivity in conditions of sunlight exposure", Archives of Ophthalmology 121(7):997-1001, July 2003 (46 subjects, Pelli-Robson chart, unobstructed sunlight). https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/the-ability-of-periorbitally-applied-antiglare-products-to-improv/ ; Yale release 2003-07-17. |
| With the grease, people saw contrast a little better | Abstract conclusion: "Eye black grease reduces glare and improves contrast sensitivity in conditions of sunlight exposure compared with the control and antiglare stickers in binocular testing." Yale release: about one level better on the chart. DeBroff in Mental Floss: the effect was "somewhat minimal". The diagram shows exactly one more row read. |
| The stickers with the logos didn't help at all | Yale release: anti-glare stickers showed no improvement in contrast sensitivity; stickers became popular because they "don't smear and some are manufactured with team logos"; "there is no benefit to their use." |
| They scored the same as a smear of petroleum jelly | Yale release and abstract: petroleum jelly was the placebo arm; no improvement for stickers or petroleum jelly. Scoreboard shows STICKERS SAME, PETROLEUM JELLY SAME (vs no product). |
| Players used to smear on burnt cork | Yale release: "Even earlier, players used to smear burnt cork on their cheeks." Wikipedia (citing Paul Lukas, ESPN): the original eye black was burnt cork ash. |
| The grease is the upgrade / It works. A little. | Summary of the above (grease beat no product and stickers; the effect is small). Wikipedia notes studies "have not conclusively proven its effectiveness" (limits: subjects knew what they wore, small samples); the script says only "a little". |

Not claimed: any number of rows or percent, that it helps play, that stickers do anything for glare, any player or team, Babe Ruth (single secondary source), any date for cork.
