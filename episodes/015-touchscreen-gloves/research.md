## Claims and sources
| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| Under the glass is a grid of tiny invisible wires that hold a little electric charge | HKUST Science Focus, Issue 016, p. 6: "the topmost layer is made up of a large network of tiny fine wires that are able to hold an electric charge." https://ebookshelf.hkust.edu.hk/flippingbook/G19107/files/basic-html/page6.html ; MakeUseOf (2021-09-11): transparent conductive layers (indium tin oxide) in a grid of rows and columns form "a large capacitor". https://www.makeuseof.com/how-do-touchscreens-work/ |
| Your skin conducts; when you touch, some charge flows into your finger; the grid feels the dip at that spot (that's your tap) | HKUST: "when your finger touches the screen, a minute amount of electricity is transferred from the network to your finger. This disturbs the original electrostatic field in the network, which is signaled by a drop in voltage at that precise location." The North Face FAQ: "Capacitive screens rely on your skin to act as an electrical conductor ... the electrical field is altered at the point of touch." https://www.thenorthface.com/en-gb/help-section/faq/products/how-do-smart-touch-gloves-work |
| Wool and leather don't conduct, the charge has nowhere to go, so to the screen you're not there | The North Face FAQ: "Most winter gloves are made from electrical insulators materials and can not complete the circuit on the capacitive screen"; on leather: "The drying process used to make leather eliminates the conducting effect of your touch." MakeUseOf: "Non-conductive items like gloves and pencils won't change the field." HKUST: capacitive screens "cannot work when we touch it whilst wearing non-conductive materials, such as gloves." |
| A sausage works (it conducts); people really tried it | Widely reported Feb 2010: South Korean phone users used snack sausages as a cold-weather stylus (Engadget 2010-02-12 "Frozen sausage as iPhone stylus"; ABC News GMA "News You Missed: Sausage Stylus"; Popular Science 2010-02 "Meat the iPhone Sausage Stylus"; The Register 2010-02-12). Why it works: any conductive object changes the field (MakeUseOf, North Face); salty, wet meat conducts. The script says only "People really tried it" (no country, no brand). |
| Touchscreen gloves have conductive thread sewn into the fingertips; the charge runs through the thread into you | The North Face FAQ: "Conductive thread can be sewn into the fingertips of your gloves, allowing the conduction of electricity on the screen." |
| Nose works (skin) | Follows from the skin-conducts claim (HKUST, North Face). Played as a gag; Orange only says "Sure." |

Simplifications: "charge flows into your finger" (HKUST's "a minute amount of electricity is transferred"); the grid is drawn as visible cyan lines for the diagram (real ones are transparent). Not claimed: which metal the thread uses, how many taps, any numbers.

Safety: no danger topic. No brand shown. The hot dog is a joke, Orange says "Please don't."
